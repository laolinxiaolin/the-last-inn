# 05 — The Ale System

The innkeeper has no sword anymore. **Ale is your only weapon. Hospitality is your magic.**

Ale isn't a resource to sell. It's a *tool for reading people* — different brews do different things to different souls. Your power isn't knowing how to fight. It's knowing what to pour.

## The Pour — the game's "combat"

Every pour is a small ceremony. The camera settles, the firelight catches the glass, the ale sounds *right* — that low, thick, satisfying pour. Serving a guest is the closest thing this game has to an action beat, and it should feel deliberate and good.

A tired man gets to do *one* satisfying thing, over and over, and it's this: sliding a perfect drink across worn wood.

## The full roster

| Brew | Character | What it gets you |
|---|---|---|
| **Common** | honest, cheap | baseline conversation |
| **The Strong** | heavy, old | stories & bravado — what they *want* you to think |
| **The Quiet** | warm, honeyed | truth from the frightened; steadier liars |
| **The Bitter** | sour, spiteful | the truth from people who've stopped caring |
| **The Dark** | stout, patient | trust from the grieving, the old, the silent |
| **The Sweet** | mead, generous | cheer, tips, embellished tales |
| **The Morning** | coffee, rare import | only for you. You don't serve it. It's yours. |
| **The Last** | a ritual pour | a blessing for someone walking into danger — affects return odds, and makes it *worse* if they don't come back |

## Drunkenness — a soft state machine

- **Warm** — happy, talkative, generous. Best returns, worst secrets.
- **Drunk** — loud, funny, sloppy. Comes back with *stories* — maybe lost the artifact, fell in a river, and it's hilarious. Failures are soft and memorable.
- **Maudlin** — the dangerous one. A maudlin guest tells you a true thing about themselves... and sometimes a true thing about *you*. This is the state that drips backstory.

Over-serving has texture, not punishment: a brawl in the corner, a marriage proposal to the goblin, a song so bad the fire dims. The night gets chaotic in a funny way, and you just ride it out. That's hospitality.

## Reading people through their drinking

The judgment system gets a second sense:

- The kid who gulps his ale like water is either starving or hiding something
- The dwarf who nurses one drink all night is disciplined — or grieving. The same drink, two stories
- The bard who orders the cheapest thing and *complains* about it is lying about his wealth — and if his wealth is a lie, so are his stories
- The hooded stranger who identifies the sweet mead on the first sip claims to be from a village a hundred miles away. *Interesting.*

## Tabs — the relationship ledger

Regulars drink on credit. The old hero's tab is so old it's a museum piece. The goblin always pays in advance and never uses his credit — he just likes having it.

Tabs are the friendship system wearing a disguise: calling in a debt is a decision; forgiving one is a *moment*. A tab never paid, after the guest doesn't come back — that's the game letting you grieve through bookkeeping.

## The House Ale — your recipe is your world

The House Ale starts plain and decent. You age it with what the world brings you: honey from the mill, smoke from the tower, something strange from the deep road. Every ingredient is a permanent decision — the recipe becomes a liquid record of everything that happened.

Late game, someone tastes it and says: *"There's grief in this one."* And they're right. The ale knows.

## The Cellar — the second room

One other room in the game, and it's the past's address. Cold, dark, kegs breathing. You go down to count stock, age the House Ale, and find things you'd forgotten — an old boot, a name carved in a beam, a locked door you've never asked about.

The cellar is where the backstory is stored, and the locked door is the endgame.

## The Toast — the game's one gesture

You can raise your glass: to a guest, to the inn, to a name. Toasting the dead at the right moment — when the courier brings the sword back, you can pour one out and stand in the silence.

The game never forces it. But the option being *there* matters. A middle-aged man knows what a raised glass means, and the game trusts him with it.

## The information economy — what each pour buys

Good stories cost good pours. Rumors, quests, warnings, a name. The bard's best tale isn't for coin — it's for the Sweet. The hooded stranger's real business isn't for the Bitter — it's for the Dark, and she knows what that costs.

Every drink is a negotiation, and the player is always choosing *what truth he can afford* — in stock, in coin, and in feeling.

## The tension — you can't pour for everyone

The cellar is finite. Ingredients come from quest outcomes — the kid clears the mill, brings back barley; the dwarf finds hops in the old tower.

Every session is a set of *attention choices*: do you spend your good dark ale on the stranger you distrust, or save it for the old hero who might open up tonight? Do you give the poor kid a free ale to steady his hands — knowing he might come home, and knowing you'll feel it twice as hard if he doesn't?

## The session bookend — brewing at closing

At the end of each night, you choose one batch to set fermenting. That's it. One decision. When you come back next session, there's a new cask ready — a little anticipation carried *across* sessions, not within them.

Perfect for a tired man: you plant a small thing, and the next time you play, it's grown.

## The full system in four words

> **Pour → read → invest → remember.**

Pour is the verb. Reading is the skill. The cellar is the economy. Memory is the reward.

It's deep, but the deepest input in the whole game is still just choosing a glass.

## Sample scene

> The hooded woman from last night is back. The caravan is found — everyone dead, silver too new. She wants to hire the kid for "one more job."
>
> You have three pours on the bar in front of you. The Common. The Bitter. The Dark.
>
> You slide the Common across. She drinks it like someone who's never had cheap ale before — and she *isn't* from the village she claimed. The Bitter would smoke her out, but she'd walk out that door and take the kid with her. The Dark might earn her trust — but the Dark is the last cask, and the old hero is in the corner tonight, and he's never once asked for it, and you've been waiting.
>
> The kid is watching you. He thinks you're deciding whether to let him go.
>
> You're deciding which truth you can afford.
