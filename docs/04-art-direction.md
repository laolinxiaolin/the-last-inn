# 04 — Art Direction

## The problem

The player stares at one room for forty hours. The art can't just be decoration — **the inn itself has to be the most interesting character in the game.**

## The headline: the inn is a living museum of your own playthrough

Every sword that comes back gets hung on the wall. Every hero who retires leaves a keepsake behind. Every death is a weapon you keep. By session 30, the walls are covered with the history of *your* game — and you remember every single piece. The player walks past a notched sword and thinks: *that's the kid from session 8.*

Their choices literally decorate the room. The inn becomes a physical record of the playthrough. This is both a systems decision and an art direction decision.

## The palette — two lights telling the story

- **Amber firelight** = the inn. Warmth, safety, comedy. The hearth is the heart of the picture.
- **Blue windowlight** = the world outside. Adventure, danger, the unknown. One window, and through it: rain, snow, fog, the treeline creeping closer as the world darkens.

The balance between the two IS the game's mood meter. Early game: fire blazing, window bright. Late game: the fire is lower, the window is all you can see, and the dark is at the glass. No UI popups. The player just *feels* it change.

## The POV — you are never on screen

The camera sits behind the bar. You see the guests across the wood, the fire beyond them. You're hands, a shoulder, a tankard sliding across the bar.

Practical (no player animation needed) and a statement: *you are the fixed point. The world comes to you.* It also makes every guest entrance a framed shot — the door swings, rain blows in, a silhouette fills the frame.

## The character style — storybook painted, with heart

Not pixel art (that lane is crowded, and the demographic has seen a million of them). Think a warm illustrated storybook — slightly exaggerated proportions, big expressive faces that read instantly from across the room.

- The exaggeration carries the comedy: the kid's sword is absurdly big, the bard's hat is a crime
- The same style can go *quiet*: a still frame of a dwarf staring at the fire can shift the whole room
- Exaggerated, but never caricature-mean. The game loves its characters, and the art has to show it

## The audio is part of the art

Fire crackle. Rain on the roof. Tankards. Creaking wood. A low, warm ambient bed that can fall silent at the dark moments — the empty sound of an inn that's waiting for someone who isn't coming back.

Sound is half the pub feeling, and it's cheap to do well.

## Readability is a design value

Parchment-and-wood UI, huge type, warm colors. No thin fonts, no tiny icons, no blue-on-blue. A tired man at midnight with the lights off should never squint. Readability is baked into the art direction.

## The pitch in one image

> A hand-painted inn at dusk, seen from behind the bar, firelight on wood, a window of blue night, and walls slowly filling with the swords of everyone you've sent out — and everyone who came home.
