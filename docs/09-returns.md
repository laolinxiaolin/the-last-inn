# 09 — Returns

## The wait

Quests take days. The player doesn't watch the quest — the player watches the door.

The game controls the rhythm: easy quests return fast, dire quests return slow, some never return. The bell becomes the game's instrument of suspense. A light step. A heavy one. A knock that isn't a guest at all.

The player learns to feel when someone isn't coming back — before the courier comes, sometimes nights before, in the way the inn gets quiet.

## Three kinds of return

### 1. The success
The door opens and they're home. They sit, they tell it — the fire warm, the ale flowing. A reward, a rumor, a story the player will hear told by bards later. The room is a little fuller tonight. This is the game's warmth, and it's earned.

### 2. The soft-fail
Alive, empty-handed, and it's *funny*. Fell in a river. Lost the artifact. Got robbed by geese. They're sheepish, the regulars laugh, the story becomes legend. The player learns: failure is survivable, and sometimes it's the best material the inn has.

### 3. The courier
No scene. The door opens. A stranger stands in the rain with a sword and a note.

> *"He said to give this to you. Said you'd know what to do with it."*

The game goes quiet. The fire pops. The note goes on the bar, and the sword goes on the wall, and the player just sits there for a moment. The wall remembers. That's the whole scene, and it's all the scene should be.

## Letters

Sometimes a letter arrives instead — written before the end, delivered after.

> *"Tell the innkeeper the sword was borrowed. Tell him I'm sorry I never paid for the ale."*

Letters are the game's way of letting the dead speak once more. They arrive on quiet evenings. They don't require a response.

## The wall remembers

Every death and every retirement hangs a weapon on the wall. The player can interact with each one — a prompt: *"You remember."* A line of memory, a drip of the past, a name.

By late game the wall is a museum of every life the player touched. Walking past a notched blade and knowing whose it was — that's the game's real inventory screen.

## Absence as design

The game never shows a death. It shows the empty chair.

The next night, someone notices: *"Where's the kid?"* The inn knows when someone's missing. The regulars go quiet. The fire seems lower. Death is a hole in the room, not a cutscene.

## The emotional rules of returns

1. **Every return is a scene** — no two returns are handled the same way; the game writes for the character, not the template.
2. **Every absence is a weight** — the game never rushes past a missing face.
3. **Sincerity is currency** — the emotional moments are rare, and they hit like punches.
4. **The player's fault is never named** — the game doesn't blame. The player's own mind does that work. It's more effective, and it's kinder.
