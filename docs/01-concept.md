# 01 — Concept

## Player profile

The target player is a middle-aged man with low energy. He grew up on NES and arcades. He's a **lapsed hardcore gamer**, not a casual — he wants to be respected, not patronized. He has 20–30 minutes after the kids are in bed, his brain is fried from work, and he wants to feel *competence*, not challenge. He will absolutely fall asleep mid-session, so the game must forgive that.

## The fantasy: the guy in the chair

The player is tired of *doing* things. The power fantasy isn't the hero — it's the **guy in the chair**: the one who decides, while the world does the doing.

> **Design principle: the world comes to you. You never leave your seat.**

## Design rules

- Sessions are 10–20 minutes. Save anywhere. Zero friction.
- No FOMO, no daily quests, no battle passes, no timers that punish.
- No fail states — or only gentle ones.
- Big readable UI — the player's eyes are 45 years old.
- One-handed controller play. Pause instantly (wife needs something).
- Depth without demand — the game can be *smart*, it just can't be *twitchy*.

## Premise

You run the only inn on the edge of the world. Adventurers stumble in — the cocky kid with a stolen sword, the grizzled dwarf, the bard who's clearly lying about everything. You size them up, hand out quests, send them off. They come back with stories, treasure, scars — or they don't come back at all.

You never leave the bar. The game is judgment, conversation, and watching stories unfold.

## The core loop

1. **Open the doors.** Guests arrive — each one hand-crafted, never a random spawn.
2. **Talk to them.** Pour ale. Ask questions. *Read them.*
3. **The quest board** has jobs — goblins at the mill, a missing caravan, the thing in the old tower.
4. **Match people to quests.** Equip them from your stock if you trust them.
5. **Close the inn.** Days pass. They come back — with coin, with stories, with scars... or not at all.
6. **Their stories become new rumors, new quests, new guests.** The world grows from what they did.

## The judgment system (the heart)

Every adventurer has visible tells (gear quality, how they hold their ale, what they brag about) and hidden truths (skill, loyalty, luck, secrets). You read them through dialogue. The cocky kid with the stolen sword is perfect for goblins and *will die* on the dragon quest. The quiet dwarf won't say a word — but he's got the hands of a man who's killed things. The bard is lying about everything, and that's fine, bards are useful.

The game never tells you the truth. You have to *feel* it. That's the skill — and it's a skill that gets better with age.

## The emotional engine (the returns)

This is where the game lives:

- The kid who comes back a man.
- The one who doesn't come back — and the courier who brings his sword back to you, because you're the last address he had.
- The old hero who retired and just wants to drink in your corner and never talk about it.
- The adventurer you sent out who comes back *changed*, and won't say what happened in the tower.

Every return is a scene. Every absence is a weight.

## The stakes: why it's the *last* inn

The other inns are gone. The region is thinning — villages quieter, roads emptier, the dark creeping closer. Your quests are holding the line. Fail enough, and the world gets darker: fewer guests, harder nights, the forest closer to your door.

There is no game over — just a slow, beautiful ending if the dark finally wins.

Underneath it all: *why are you here?* What happened to the hero you used to be? That's the slow-burn story the game tells in drips, over many sessions.

## Low-energy design specifics

- One session = one evening at the inn. 15–20 minutes. Save anywhere, auto-save at dawn.
- No fail states, no timers, no twitch. The hardest input is choosing dialogue.
- Big readable UI, one-handed controller play, pause instantly.
- A "quiet night" is a feature: sometimes nobody comes, and you just pour yourself an ale and listen to the rain. That's not downtime — that's the game being kind to you.

## Sample evening (from the pitch)

> Rain. A kid comes in with a rusty sword and eyes too big for his face. A dwarf sits in the corner and says nothing. A woman in a hood pays for a room in silver that's *too new*.
>
> The board has three jobs: goblins at the mill, a missing caravan, the thing in the old tower.
>
> You talk to the kid — eager, honest, terrible. You give him the goblins. The woman — you can't read her, and that's the problem. You give her the caravan. The dwarf won't talk, so you pour him a free ale, and he gives you one sentence: *"The tower's not the problem. The tower's a door."* You send him to the tower.
>
> Three days later: the kid comes back with a goblin ear and a grin. The dwarf comes back with nothing, and drinks until closing, and says nothing. The woman never comes back — but the caravan is found, everyone dead, and the silver in their pockets is too new. Now you know what she was. And she knows you know.
