# 11 — Progression

## No numbers, no skill trees

The player never sees a stat, a bar, or a level. Progression is the inn itself — it gets warmer, fuller, more alive. And then the dark comes, and progression inverts: the inn gets quieter. Both directions are the game.

## The inn grows

- **The hearth** — bigger, warmer. More guests come in from the cold. The fire is also the mood; a great hearth is a statement.
- **The cellar** — more kegs, better brews, the ability to age. The ale system deepens.
- **The sign** — reputation. Travelers come from farther. Someone says: *"I came three valleys because my grandmother told me about this inn."*
- **The bard's corner** — Fenwick's job. Stories, songs, rumors. The corner changes the room.
- **The stable** — more adventurers can rest → more quest capacity, deeper relationships with the cast.
- **The rooms** — guests stay longer. Long stays mean long conversations. The narrative deepens.

Upgrades are choices, not chores. Each one costs coin and takes time (a season, a winter) — the game has patience, and so should the player.

## Reputation is earned, never counted

The inn is famous when someone *says* it is. No meter. The reputation is in the dialogue, the letters, the travelers who arrive with a name for the House Ale.

## The wall is the meta-progression

The museum of swords and keepsakes is the player's real progress bar — a physical record of every life they touched. The player doesn't level up. The player looks at the wall and *remembers*. That's advancement.

## The House Ale is the world record

Every ingredient added is permanent. The recipe becomes a liquid history of the playthrough. Late-game, someone tastes it and tells you what your own game has been. The ale knows.

## Tabs and relationships are the social progression

- The old hero's tab: a museum piece
- The goblin's unused credit: he just likes having it
- Forgiving a debt: a moment
- Calling one in: a decision

Relationships are the deepest progression in the game, and they're tracked the way a real innkeeper tracks them: in who owes what, who's been gone too long, and who's still coming through the door.

## What the dark can take

Loss is the game's one real threat — and it's *felt*, never shown:

- A village falls. Its lights go out in the window. Its people stop coming
- A regular stops showing up. The chair stays empty
- The stable stands empty. The bell is quiet

The player can lose progress — not as a punishment, but as weather. The game is honest about what the world does.

## The player's own arc

The player's real progression is internal: the backstory drip (see [03](03-backstory.md)), the wall of memories, the moment when the door in the cellar starts to matter. The inn's story and the player's story are the same progression, told two ways.
