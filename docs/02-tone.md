# 02 — Tone

## The headline

**70% funny, 30% dark — but the 30% is what you remember.**

## Why this ratio

The player has lived real darkness: dead parents, divorce or the quiet version of it, friends who died too young. He doesn't need a game to be grim to feel real — grimdark reads as *homework* to a tired man.

But humor is how men his age actually process pain. They don't sit and emote — they crack a joke about it at the wake. **The comedy isn't the opposite of the sadness. It's the vehicle for it.**

## The rules of the tone

### 1. Comedy comes from character, not jokes

No pun-factory dialogue. The humor lives in the gap between what adventurers think they are and what they actually are:

- The bard who claims he slew a dragon — he hid in a barrel, the dragon tripped over the barrel, everyone assumed
- The "Chosen One" chosen by three prophecies that contradict each other
- The wizard who is just a guy with a stick and excellent posture

### 2. Darkness comes from absence, never gore

Nobody gets graphically murdered. People just... don't come back. The courier arrives with a sword and a note: *"He said to give this to you. Said you'd know what to do with it."* You hang it over the bar. You don't say anything.

The player's own imagination does the violence. It's more adult, and it hits harder.

### 3. The humor has a middle-aged lens

A kid swears "I will reclaim my honor!" and your options include: *"You'll be lucky to reclaim the deposit on that sword."*

Every line should sound like something a tired man would actually say — dry, deflective, understated.

### 4. Sincerity is currency — spend it rarely

When a character says something genuinely sincere, it should feel like a punch to the chest. If everyone's sincere, no one is.

The dwarf who says nothing for three sessions, and then, when you're closing up, says one sentence about his daughter. That's it. That's the whole scene.

### 5. Never punch down

You laugh at the kid — but you also *root* for him. The game loves its characters. The comedy is the warmth. The darkness is the truth. Both are true at once.

## The structural arc

The ratio should mirror the player's own life: early sessions are funnier, lighter — a warm pub comedy. But as the world darkens and the backstory unfolds, the ratio shifts, slowly, invisibly.

By the late game, the funny moments are rarer and they *hurt* when they land, because you know what's coming. The game gets heavier as you go deeper. You don't notice it happening — until one night you realize the inn is quieter than it used to be.

## Sample moments

> **Funny:** A goblin walks in. Polite. Pays for a room in advance. Compliments your ale. Writes a thank-you note on the way out. He is the best guest you've had in three weeks. You watch him leave and think about the other guests, and pour yourself a drink.

> **Dark:** The old hero sleeps in the corner every night, and you realize you've started counting his drinks — to make sure he's still alive. The way he used to count yours, back when you were the kid with the rusty sword.

## The tone in one sentence

> A warm pub comedy that slowly reveals it's a story about getting older, losing people, and the quiet dignity of keeping the fire lit for the next kid who walks in.
